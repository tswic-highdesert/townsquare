# The problem Town Square exists to solve

Written for the community, not for engineers. Usable as the front of the deck, a
standalone handout, or the body of a grant application.

---

## The short version

**Russellville has no shared front door.**

Everything a resident might want to know about their town exists somewhere. The
council decided something Tuesday. A nonprofit needs volunteers Saturday. A new
business opened on Main Street. Someone recorded a podcast about the river.

None of it is in the same place. Most of it never reaches the person who would
have cared.

So every organization in town builds its own audience from scratch, over and
over, and pays for the privilege. And every resident assembles their own
patchwork of Facebook groups, word of mouth, and luck, then still misses most of
what is happening.

That is not four problems. It is one problem wearing four coats.

---

## What people actually say

On August 6, 2026, roughly a dozen community members sat in a room at the
Co-Create Innovation Hub and were asked, one at a time, to name a problem
technology might solve. Before Town Square was ever mentioned, people named:

- People are not involved.
- People do not know what is happening.
- It costs money just to send an email.

Arkansas Tech University had already documented the same thing. Summarizing an
earlier Community Health Needs-Finding Workshop sponsored by AR-NETWORK, ATU
listed the needs its community partners identified:

> improved access to information, stronger communication about available
> services, transportation coordination, resource mapping, data collection

That list was written by a university, about this town, before we walked in. It
is worth pausing on, because it means nobody has to be convinced the problem is
real. It is already on the record.

---

## Why the usual answers do not work

**Facebook groups.** Rented land. There is an algorithm between an organization
and the people who explicitly asked to hear from it, and the terms change
whenever someone else decides they should. A group that took three years to
build can lose its reach in a week.

**Local news.** Thinning, here and everywhere. What remains cannot cover a
school board, a quorum court, a planning commission, and forty nonprofits.

**The city website.** Built to satisfy a legal requirement to publish, not to
help a resident understand. A PDF posted on time is compliance. It is not
communication.

**Email lists.** Expensive, one-way, and siloed by design. Every organization
pays separately to reach an overlapping set of the same people, and none of
those lists ever help a resident discover an organization they had not already
heard of.

**Another app.** Every community app so far is a product people consume. It
lives or dies on whether one organization can keep feeding it forever. Most die.

---

## What we think the answer is

Not a better newsletter. **Infrastructure the community builds on.**

Town Square has three layers.

**1. Aggregation.** What is happening. News, events, notices, pulled together in
one place.

**2. Directory and discovery.** Not just a list of businesses. A way to find the
thing you did not know to look for, across businesses, articles, podcasts, and
organizations. 741 real Russellville listings are already loaded.

**3. The build layer.** This is the part that makes it different.

In 2026, a person with an idea and an afternoon can build a small working
application. What they cannot do is give it a home, an audience, or a way to
connect to anything else in town. Town Square is that foundation and that front
door.

A resident builds something and plugs it in. A map of where the food pantries
are. A tracker for when the council takes up rezoning. A ranking of the best
tamales in town. Town Square does not build those things. Town Square is what
they stand on and how the town finds them.

And each one leaves something behind. A creation publishes structured records
back into Town Square, so the next person's app starts with data the last
person's app produced.

---

## Why this compounds

A normal community app gets more valuable the more people read it, which means
it needs one organization to feed it forever, and it dies when that organization
gets tired.

Town Square gets more valuable **the more people build on it.**

Ten builders make it useful. Fifty make it hard to replace. And because each
creation leaves structured data behind, the fiftieth builder starts from
somewhere the first one could not have reached.

That is the bet: that a town's information problem is not solved by one org
publishing harder, but by giving everybody who already wants to help a place to
plug in.

---

## Who it belongs to

Nobody should own the town's front door.

Town Square is **open source**, so another town can take it, run it, and adapt
it without asking permission. Governance sits with a **local Town Square
committee**, not a company and not a person, which is also what an adopting town
stands up for itself.

Residency is verified by **vouching**: residents vouch for residents. We store
that someone is verified and never store where they live.

Moderation is light. This is a neutral substrate, not an editorial product. Every
record is versioned, attributed, and revertable, which is what lets it stay that
way.

---

## The state of it

This is not a concept. As of August 2026 the platform runs, with identity,
vouching, the directory, the creation sandbox, the builder tooling, and the
review pipeline all built and tested. Russellville is town one.

What it needs now is not code. It needs the town.
