Town Square

A town square the town actually owns, built so anyone who lives here can add to it.

Prepared for the ATU CCEAO community coalition · August 2026
Start with the problem

The problem

Russellville does not have a town square anymore.

Everything a resident might want to know about their town exists somewhere.

  • City hall The council decided something Tuesday.
  • A nonprofit Volunteers needed Saturday.
  • Main Street A new business opened.
  • Somebody’s feed A podcast about the river.

None of it is in the same place.

Most of it never reaches the person who would have cared.

That is not four problems. It is one problem showing up as different symptoms.

What people actually say

On August 6, 2026, community members sat in a room at the Co-Create Innovation Hub and were asked, one at a time, to name a problem technology might solve. Before Town Square was ever mentioned, people named these.

  1. “

    People are not involved.

  2. “

    People do not know what is happening.

  3. “

    It costs money just to send an email, and no organization here has the budget to communicate effectively in the landscape we actually live in.

Arkansas Tech University has documented the same thing, summarizing an earlier AR-NETWORK needs-finding workshop.

  • Improved access to information
  • Stronger communication about available services
  • Transportation coordination
  • Resource mapping
  • Data collection

We have been hearing a version of this for a decade, and louder every year since Covid. Ask the same question in any town in America and you will get these same answers back. That is not a coincidence, and it is not a Russellville failing.

Nobody needs convincing that the problem is real. We have all been living in it. What has been missing is a solution anyone has actually dialed in.

How we got here

A community’s most valuable asset is its attention.

For most of a century that attention had somewhere to live.

What we had
  • The paper landed on the porch.
  • The radio was on in the truck.

That infrastructure was analog, physical, local, and imperfect, and it worked. If something mattered, it reached you, and the institution that carried it lived in the same town you did.

We gave that away.

Who we gave it to

Not to another local institution. We handed it to Zuckerberg and friends, to companies two thousand miles from here that rent our attention back to us and decide, day to day, who sees what.

A nonprofit in Russellville now has to buy access to people who already asked to hear from it.

The old infrastructure did not simply fade out. It was replaced by something that was never built to serve a town in the first place.

And it was doing two jobs, not one

The paper was the town’s megaphone and the town’s archive.

01

It reached people today.

Whatever mattered on a Tuesday got in front of the town on Tuesday, without anyone having to go looking for it.

What replaced it does this job badly.

02

It wrote down what happened for tomorrow.

A shelf of back issues is a town keeping its own record, one ordinary day at a time, without calling it a history project.

What replaced it does not do this job at all. A Facebook post from 2019 is functionally unfindable.

We did not just trade a distribution channel. We traded an archive for a machine designed to forget.

The usual answers

Why the usual answers do not work

Each of these has been tried, here and in towns like ours. They do not fail vaguely. They fail in specific, nameable ways.

Facebook groups

Rented land

There is an algorithm between an organization and the people who explicitly asked to hear from it, and the terms change whenever someone else decides they should. A group that took three years to build can lose its reach in a week.

Local news

Thinning everywhere

Thinning here, and thinning everywhere else too. What remains cannot cover a school board, a quorum court, a planning commission, and forty nonprofits.

The city website

Built for the statute

Built to satisfy a legal requirement to publish, not to help a resident understand.

A PDF posted on time is compliance. It is not communication.

Email lists

Expensive and siloed

One-way and siloed by design. Every organization pays separately to reach an overlapping set of the same people, and none of those lists ever help a resident discover an organization they had not already heard of.

Another app

Most die

Every community app so far is a product people consume. It lives or dies on whether one organization can keep feeding it forever.

What we think the answer is

Not a better newsletter. Something the whole community can build on.

Town Square is built in three layers, and each one asks more of the town than the one before it. That order is deliberate. It has to be useful before anyone is asked to participate, and useful and busy before anyone is asked to build.

1

Bring in what already exists

We start by pulling the town's existing signals into one place. Business listings, news sites, event calendars, public notices, podcasts. Everything already out there and already worth having, gathered.

Nobody has to change what they do for this to work.

No organization has to adopt a new platform, learn a new tool, or move their calendar. We come to the information where it already lives. On day one, before a single resident signs up, there is already something worth walking up to.

2

Let the town add what only people can add

Residents add what no scraper will ever find. What is actually going on, what is worth going to, what a place is really like. Reviews, recommendations, notices, corrections, answers.

Then the difference that matters, and it is the whole difference.

We do not sell ads, so we have no reason to hide anything from you.

We have no ad business to protect. If you asked to see it, you see it.

Why an ad feed has to hide things

An ad-funded feed makes money by controlling the slot in front of you, so it has to decide what you do not see in order to sell that space back to the people who made it. That is why the nonprofit you followed on purpose has to pay to reach you. It is not a bug in the system, it is the system.

Why removing ads is not enough

Worth saying plainly, because it is the trap. Ranking purely by engagement recreates the same problem even without ads, since outrage is engaging. Taking away the ad motive does not, on its own, get you a place that treats you honestly.

So the rule is this. Discovery only ever adds to what you asked for. It never takes away.

3

Let anyone build on it

In 2026 a person with an idea and an afternoon can build a real working app. What they cannot do is give it a home, an audience, or any connection to anything else in their town. That is what this is.

Watchdog

Civic

Delight

Not everything has to be civic. Some of it just has to be worth arguing about.

Commerce

A marketplace that belongs to the town, instead of one more thing happening in somebody else's app.

Service

Who needs help Saturday

Every local nonprofit posting volunteer shifts in one place, free, instead of each one paying to email the same 200 people.

Their own thing

A podcast, a cause, a business

Promote it, grow it, and yes, make money doing it. That is allowed and it is supposed to be.

We are not building those. We are building the foundation that makes them easy to build in the AI era, and the place where the town finds them.

One rule

Every creation has to leave something behind. Whatever it learns about the town gets contributed back, so the next person's app starts with everything the last person's app figured out.

The kind of thing a resident does with this

Someone asks the city how many license plate readers are up on Arkansas Avenue, and never gets a straight number. Three weekends later there are 41 of them on a map, the map lives on Town Square, and anyone in town can add the one they drove past this morning.

She had not written code before April. She works the desk at the library. What she needed was never a developer. It was somewhere to put the thing she made, and neighbors who would actually find it.

That is the whole shape of this layer. The town supplies the curiosity. Town Square supplies the ground it can stand on.

The state of it

This part is built and running today.

  1. 01 Connect

    A resident points their own AI tool at Town Square.

  2. 02 Build

    They build against a stable interface, so what they make works with the town's record instead of around it.

  3. 03 Submit

    Every submission is reviewed before it goes live, and so is every update after that.

Why it compounds

It gets more valuable the more people build on it.

A normal community app gets more valuable the more people read it. That is why it needs one organization to feed it forever, and why it dies when that organization gets tired. This one works the other way around.

Because each creation leaves what it learned behind, the fiftieth builder starts from somewhere the first one could not have reached.

That is the bet. A town’s information problem is not solved by one organization publishing harder. It is solved by giving everybody who already wants to help a place to plug in.

And then there is the part nobody is asking for yet

A town’s memory is currently stored in people. It leaves when they do.

  • Which contractor actually shows up.
  • Which road floods first when the river comes up.
  • What that building was before it was the insurance office, and before that.
  • Why the school board voted the way it did in 2019, and why it still matters.
  • Who to call.
  • How to get help when you are behind on a utility bill.

Every town in America is permanently re-learning things it already knew.

There are people working on this. Historical societies, the library’s local history room, volunteers scanning photographs, someone recording their grandmother before it is too late. That work is real, and it is mostly done by one or two dedicated people, into a filing cabinet, and it stops when they stop.

Here is what changes

Town Square does not have to run a history project. It produces the record as a byproduct of being useful today.

  1. Today’s agenda → Next decade’s primary source.
  2. A directory kept for twenty years → A record of every business that opened and closed, and what stood where.
  3. An events calendar → A record of what the town cared about, year over year.

None of that requires anybody to decide to preserve it. It accumulates because the thing was built to be useful on a Tuesday.

Most of what a community loses is not lost because it was unimportant. It is lost because nobody was writing it down at the time in a form that survived.

The same loop, pointed at the past

Once that is true, the build layer stops being about apps and starts being about acceleration.

  1. 01

    An oral history recorder

    Somebody builds one, and the recordings attach to the people, places, and businesses already in the record.

    Leaves behind: voices, attached to the people and places they are about

  2. 02

    The historical society’s photographs

    Somebody digitizes them and pins each one to an address that is already there.

    Leaves behind: images, attached to real addresses

  3. 03

    A what-used-to-be-here tool

    Somebody builds it and it works immediately, because twenty years of directory data is sitting there waiting for it.

    Leaves behind: a timeline for every address in town

  4. 04

    A cemetery survey

    Somebody walks it, and it connects to obituaries, which connect to families, which connect to the businesses they ran.

    Leaves behind: the people, tied back to everything above

Each one starts with what the last one left behind. Nobody had to fund a project. It is the same loop, pointed at the town’s memory instead of its calendar.

We are not going to pretend we know where that ends up. That is sort of the point.

Ownership

Nobody should own a town square.

We did not pick the name to be cute.

In the early days, Facebook described itself as the digital town square. They were right about what they were building.

They were just also the ones who owned it, and they sell it.

This is not an argument against free enterprise. Businesses should make money.

But a town square is not supposed to be a product. It is the one place where a community sees itself, and when that place has been bought and paid for by interests two thousand miles away, the community does not get to decide what happens in it anymore.

Somebody else does, on a quarterly basis, for reasons that have nothing to do with this town.

And this has already cost us something.

This part is not theoretical.

When the only reliable way to reach your neighbors is a channel you have to pay for, the organizations that get squeezed out first are the ones with the smallest budgets. Those are often the ones doing the most urgent work.

  • The crisis line.
  • The shelter with beds open tonight.
  • The clinic with a closing enrollment window.
  • The warming center.
  • The pantry.

A nonprofit that cannot get a message out is not merely inconvenienced. There is a person on the other end of that message who needed it, and consequences that follow when it never arrives. Everyone who works in this field has watched it happen and can name the case.

The wider damage is slower and harder to see. Civic participation thins. People stop knowing their neighbors, stop showing up, stop trusting institutions they no longer hear from directly. That is a real deterioration of civic fabric, and it has real costs.

A research ask for Arkansas Tech

We cannot currently tell you what those costs are here, in numbers. Nobody has measured it in this community.

That is one of the things we would like ATU to help with: a study that quantifies what the loss of a shared local information channel has actually done to Russellville, and what could be done about it.

Naming the harm precisely is the first step to fixing it, and it is exactly the kind of question a university should be answering.

So here is how this one is held.

  1. 01 Open source

    Another town can take it, run it, and adapt it without asking anyone. Russellville is town one, not the only town.

  2. 02 A local committee

    Governance sits with a local Town Square committee, not a company and not a person, which is also what an adopting town stands up for itself.

  3. 03 Vouching

    Residency is verified by residents vouching for residents. We store that someone is verified and never store where they live.

  4. 04 Light touch

    This is a neutral place, not a publisher. Every record is versioned, attributed, and reversible, which is what lets it stay that way.

None of that makes it un-ownable forever. It makes it owned by the town instead of by us.

The idea the whole thing turns on

Students build creations on Town Square, never Town Square itself.

One constraint, and it buys four things at once.

  1. 01

    A structural non-blocking guarantee

    Creations are sandboxed, so a team that stalls cannot slow the platform down. That is an architectural property, not a promise about student quality.

  2. 02

    Free accountability

    “Did the team deliver” has a binary, dated answer: did their exact version pass review. The academic deadline and the platform’s gate are the same event, and faculty get a grading artifact out of it.

  3. 03

    Thesis validation

    Town Square’s whole bet is that residents will build on it. Student teams building on it is the first real evidence, and it arrives with a public showcase in April.

  4. 04

    Nothing is orphaned

    ATU’s own partner guide is explicit that students and faculty own nothing once the course ends. Every project here runs on infrastructure that is already maintained, so there is no abandoned system in April.

The calendar, which ATU sets

September to April, ending in front of the town.

  1. Late Aug to early Sept 2026

    Student teams are formed and projects are selected.

  2. By mid-September

    Selected project partners are contacted.

  3. September to April

The capstone slate

Seven projects, and none of them start from zero.

Each one answers a need Arkansas Tech documented itself, and each one is already written as a complete submission. Four we recommend sending. Three depend on a decision that is not ours alone to make.

The recommended slate

Four submissions, three documented needs, three different technical profiles. None carries political or schedule risk.

  1. 01 Recommended

    Civic Meeting Tracker

    City Council, the School Board, the Pope County Quorum Court, and the Planning Commission each post agendas on their own site, on their own schedule, usually as PDFs. Decisions are effectively invisible until after they are made.

    Students build Automated ingest from at least four governing bodies, a normalization layer that turns inconsistent PDFs into typed records, full-text and topic search, subscriptions that notify a resident when a subject they follow reaches any agenda, and plain-language summaries.

    Documented need
    Improved access to information
    Technical profile
    Medium ceiling, low risk. Resilient ingest against sources that change without notice, document parsing, and deciding how a summary should behave when it is not confident.
  2. 02 Recommended

    Community Services and Volunteer Board

    Every nonprofit in town keeps its own mailing list, its own calendar, and its own social presence, and pays in money or volunteer hours to rebuild an audience from scratch. A resident looking for help, or for somewhere to volunteer Saturday, has no single place to look.

    Students build Verified organization profiles, posting for services, events, and volunteer needs, browse and filter, resident saves and reminders, and a posting flow simple enough that nonprofit staff need no intermediary. Target is eight to ten real organizations posting by the April showcase.

    Documented need
    Communication about services. Resource mapping.
    Technical profile
    Medium ceiling, lowest risk. Multi-tenant modeling, authorization, and real user research. The heaviest risk here is adoption rather than engineering.
  3. 03 Recommended

    Community Discovery Engine

    Search only helps a resident who already knows what to type, so the entire long tail of local life stays invisible to anyone not already connected to it. Newcomers feel that hardest.

    Students build Unified search across businesses, articles, podcasts, and events in one ranked set, semantic search in a resident's own words, automatic tagging, a related-items engine, a recommendation surface for browsing with no query at all, and an evaluation harness with a labeled relevance set.

    Documented need
    Resource mapping. Data collection.
    Technical profile
    High ceiling, low risk. The most technically ambitious on the list. Retrieval and ranking across types, embeddings, cold start, and defending a metric rather than asserting one.
  4. 07 Recommended

    Measuring the Local Information Gap and What It Costs

    Everyone doing community work here can describe the information gap anecdotally. Nobody has measured it. Without a local baseline, every intervention including this one is a guess, and no funder can be shown a number.

    Students build A collection platform where web, assisted phone, and paper all land in one dataset, a reporting tool where nonprofits log what they tried to communicate and what it cost, a record linkage and analysis pipeline that never stores identifying information, and a published instrument the town can run again in two years.

    The only proposal that produces evidence rather than software, and the one to push hardest. It is interdisciplinary, pairing computing with sociology. IRB review is a hard dependency and a real schedule risk, so it has to start in the fall.

    Documented need
    All of them, by measuring the gap itself.
    Technical profile
    High ceiling. Multi-modal collection, record linkage with no shared identifier, and privacy engineering defended to a real ethics review.

Conditional, and why

Three good projects held back by something other than the code. Each one is written and ready if the condition clears.

  1. 04 On hold

    Community Transportation Coordination

    Documented need
    Transportation coordination
    Technical profile
    High ceiling, medium risk

    Senior services, medical transport, volunteer driver networks, and social service programs each handle a slice of local transportation, each with its own eligibility rules, hours, and request process. None of it is visible in one place, and the people most affected are the least equipped to navigate it.

    Students build A provider directory, an eligibility engine that returns only the options a resident actually qualifies for, a request and routing flow, proxy requests so caseworkers and family can act on someone's behalf, accessibility as a hard requirement, and de-identified demand reporting that shows funders unmet need.

    Held unless Russellville Housing Authority co-signs as a second partner. With that co-signature it is the strongest proposal on the list. Without it, we would be describing a problem we have not verified from the inside.

  2. 05 Judgment call

    Public Surveillance Infrastructure Transparency Map

    Documented need
    Data collection
    Technical profile
    Medium ceiling

    Automated license plate reader cameras operate throughout Russellville. Their locations, retention periods, and data-sharing arrangements are all public record, but nobody has ever assembled them anywhere a resident could read them.

    Students build A records-request workflow tool, a document repository with structured extraction from scanned records, an interactive map where every entry links to the record supporting it, a plain-language policy layer, and a provenance model that makes it architecturally impossible to display an unverified claim as verified.

    Politically warm, and the call is a judgment call rather than a technical one. It attaches a surveillance story to Town Square's launch and to ATU's. The framing is deliberately neutral, about public records rather than a position on the technology, and the critical path runs through government response times rather than through the code.

  3. 06 Optional

    Community Builder Toolkit

    Documented need
    Developer tooling, not a community need
    Technical profile
    Medium to high ceiling, low risk

    The machinery that lets a resident publish their own application onto Town Square already works. The on-ramp does not. Today a first-time builder has to assemble their own project structure and learn the manifest format by reading a specification.

    Students build A command-line tool that scaffolds a project, runs it locally, validates it against the platform contract, and submits it for publication, plus three or four starter templates, error messages written for a novice, and a test suite for a tool whose output is other projects.

    Optional, and the weakest fit for a program framed around community needs, because the users are developers rather than residents. It is the best pure software engineering project on the list, and the only one that touches core platform code. That work is purely additive, so late delivery breaks nothing that exists today.

What every one of them says

01

Students build on a running platform, not from zero.

Town Square supplies hosting, authentication, a documented SDK, a design system, and a code review pipeline on day one, along with real data: a directory of 741 verified Russellville listings, plus aggregated local articles, events, and notices. Teams spend two semesters on their actual problem instead of spending the fall building scaffolding.

02

Nothing becomes an orphan.

ATU's partner guide is explicit that students and faculty own nothing after the course ends. Every project here is a sandboxed creation on infrastructure that is already maintained, and the partner owns hosting during the course and after it. There is no abandoned system in April, which answers the availability of resources criterion directly.

None of these are sketches. All seven exist as finished documents with form-ready answers for every question the Project Idea Form asks: the problem, the users, the outcomes, the feature set, the technical challenges, the resources supplied, and an honest account of what could go wrong.

Ready to submit as written.
