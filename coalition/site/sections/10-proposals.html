The capstone slate

Seven projects, and none of them start from zero.

Each one answers a need Arkansas Tech documented itself, and each one is already written as a complete submission. Four we recommend sending. Three depend on a decision that is not ours alone to make.

The recommended slate

Four submissions, three documented needs, three different technical profiles. None carries political or schedule risk.

  1. 01 Recommended

    Civic Meeting Tracker

    City Council, the School Board, the Pope County Quorum Court, and the Planning Commission each post agendas on their own site, on their own schedule, usually as PDFs. Decisions are effectively invisible until after they are made.

    Students build Automated ingest from at least four governing bodies, a normalization layer that turns inconsistent PDFs into typed records, full-text and topic search, subscriptions that notify a resident when a subject they follow reaches any agenda, and plain-language summaries.

    Documented need
    Improved access to information
    Technical profile
    Medium ceiling, low risk. Resilient ingest against sources that change without notice, document parsing, and deciding how a summary should behave when it is not confident.
  2. 02 Recommended

    Community Services and Volunteer Board

    Every nonprofit in town keeps its own mailing list, its own calendar, and its own social presence, and pays in money or volunteer hours to rebuild an audience from scratch. A resident looking for help, or for somewhere to volunteer Saturday, has no single place to look.

    Students build Verified organization profiles, posting for services, events, and volunteer needs, browse and filter, resident saves and reminders, and a posting flow simple enough that nonprofit staff need no intermediary. Target is eight to ten real organizations posting by the April showcase.

    Documented need
    Communication about services. Resource mapping.
    Technical profile
    Medium ceiling, lowest risk. Multi-tenant modeling, authorization, and real user research. The heaviest risk here is adoption rather than engineering.
  3. 03 Recommended

    Community Discovery Engine

    Search only helps a resident who already knows what to type, so the entire long tail of local life stays invisible to anyone not already connected to it. Newcomers feel that hardest.

    Students build Unified search across businesses, articles, podcasts, and events in one ranked set, semantic search in a resident's own words, automatic tagging, a related-items engine, a recommendation surface for browsing with no query at all, and an evaluation harness with a labeled relevance set.

    Documented need
    Resource mapping. Data collection.
    Technical profile
    High ceiling, low risk. The most technically ambitious on the list. Retrieval and ranking across types, embeddings, cold start, and defending a metric rather than asserting one.
  4. 07 Recommended

    Measuring the Local Information Gap and What It Costs

    Everyone doing community work here can describe the information gap anecdotally. Nobody has measured it. Without a local baseline, every intervention including this one is a guess, and no funder can be shown a number.

    Students build A collection platform where web, assisted phone, and paper all land in one dataset, a reporting tool where nonprofits log what they tried to communicate and what it cost, a record linkage and analysis pipeline that never stores identifying information, and a published instrument the town can run again in two years.

    The only proposal that produces evidence rather than software, and the one to push hardest. It is interdisciplinary, pairing computing with sociology. IRB review is a hard dependency and a real schedule risk, so it has to start in the fall.

    Documented need
    All of them, by measuring the gap itself.
    Technical profile
    High ceiling. Multi-modal collection, record linkage with no shared identifier, and privacy engineering defended to a real ethics review.

Conditional, and why

Three good projects held back by something other than the code. Each one is written and ready if the condition clears.

  1. 04 On hold

    Community Transportation Coordination

    Documented need
    Transportation coordination
    Technical profile
    High ceiling, medium risk

    Senior services, medical transport, volunteer driver networks, and social service programs each handle a slice of local transportation, each with its own eligibility rules, hours, and request process. None of it is visible in one place, and the people most affected are the least equipped to navigate it.

    Students build A provider directory, an eligibility engine that returns only the options a resident actually qualifies for, a request and routing flow, proxy requests so caseworkers and family can act on someone's behalf, accessibility as a hard requirement, and de-identified demand reporting that shows funders unmet need.

    Held unless Russellville Housing Authority co-signs as a second partner. With that co-signature it is the strongest proposal on the list. Without it, we would be describing a problem we have not verified from the inside.

  2. 05 Judgment call

    Public Surveillance Infrastructure Transparency Map

    Documented need
    Data collection
    Technical profile
    Medium ceiling

    Automated license plate reader cameras operate throughout Russellville. Their locations, retention periods, and data-sharing arrangements are all public record, but nobody has ever assembled them anywhere a resident could read them.

    Students build A records-request workflow tool, a document repository with structured extraction from scanned records, an interactive map where every entry links to the record supporting it, a plain-language policy layer, and a provenance model that makes it architecturally impossible to display an unverified claim as verified.

    Politically warm, and the call is a judgment call rather than a technical one. It attaches a surveillance story to Town Square's launch and to ATU's. The framing is deliberately neutral, about public records rather than a position on the technology, and the critical path runs through government response times rather than through the code.

  3. 06 Optional

    Community Builder Toolkit

    Documented need
    Developer tooling, not a community need
    Technical profile
    Medium to high ceiling, low risk

    The machinery that lets a resident publish their own application onto Town Square already works. The on-ramp does not. Today a first-time builder has to assemble their own project structure and learn the manifest format by reading a specification.

    Students build A command-line tool that scaffolds a project, runs it locally, validates it against the platform contract, and submits it for publication, plus three or four starter templates, error messages written for a novice, and a test suite for a tool whose output is other projects.

    Optional, and the weakest fit for a program framed around community needs, because the users are developers rather than residents. It is the best pure software engineering project on the list, and the only one that touches core platform code. That work is purely additive, so late delivery breaks nothing that exists today.

What every one of them says

01

Students build on a running platform, not from zero.

Town Square supplies hosting, authentication, a documented SDK, a design system, and a code review pipeline on day one, along with real data: a directory of 741 verified Russellville listings, plus aggregated local articles, events, and notices. Teams spend two semesters on their actual problem instead of spending the fall building scaffolding.

02

Nothing becomes an orphan.

ATU's partner guide is explicit that students and faculty own nothing after the course ends. Every project here is a sandboxed creation on infrastructure that is already maintained, and the partner owns hosting during the course and after it. There is no abandoned system in April, which answers the availability of resources criterion directly.

None of these are sketches. All seven exist as finished documents with form-ready answers for every question the Project Idea Form asks: the problem, the users, the outcomes, the feature set, the technical challenges, the resources supplied, and an honest account of what could go wrong.

Ready to submit as written.
