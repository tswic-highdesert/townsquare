Civic, notices, and emergency

City hall, the notice ladder, and the one screen that is allowed to take over the phone. The emergency register exists so that every ordinary day can stay calm, which means most of this section is about restraint: four severities and no more, one alarm colour used once per screen, and a default subscription that will not wake anyone up for a planning commission agenda.

Four, not seven. Every severity level you add is a level someone has to learn, and the ones in the middle are the ones nobody can tell apart. A resident does not need to know the difference between a level 3 and a level 4; they need to know four things, in this order: is this for me, is it happening now, do I have to do something, and does it wake me up. Four levels answer that with one question each. Informational is news you would want anyway. Advisory is a condition to be aware of. Urgent changes what you do today. Emergency changes what you do in the next ten minutes. Seven levels would only give the middle three a false precision no source actually supplies – the National Weather Service publishes watch, advisory, and warning; the water utility publishes an order or nothing. We are mapping upstream vocabularies, not inventing one, and the ladder has to be lossy in the safe direction. When a source is ambiguous we round down, then let the resident raise their own threshold. Colour is never the carrier: each notice states its severity in words above the headline, because the whole ladder has to survive a screenshot, a screen reader, and colour blindness.

The dismissal is the hard part. An X in the top corner trains people to close the screen before they read it, and it puts the escape at the exact spot a thumb lands when a phone buzzes at 11pm. So there is no X. The dismissal sits at the bottom, after the instruction and after the source, it is a full sentence, and it says what it does: hide this until it changes. Any update from the source brings it straight back, and hiding never unsubscribes. The one action is one action, always: a second button here is a decision, and a decision at 11pm is a delay. Everything else on the screen is text you can act on without tapping anything, because the most likely outcome is that the phone gets put down and the person moves. After it clears, the takeover does not just vanish – it becomes a calm card with the whole sequence in it, because the question the next morning is always "what actually happened last night", and if we do not answer it the answer comes from a rumour.

Attribution becomes typographic. The brand survives as five words in the quietest line on the screen, and the mark comes back on the what-happened card where the register is neutral again.

The resolution is removal, not redrawing. The mark's job is to tell you which app you are in, and on a takeover that is the one thing the person already knows – their own phone woke them up. Worse, the mark at the attribution point implies we are the source. We are not. The National Weather Service issued the warning; we carried it. Putting our logo where the issuing agency's name belongs is a small credit-taking that becomes a real problem the first time an alert is wrong. So the takeover carries no mark at all, the source agency gets the emphatic line, and "relayed by Town Square" is a clause in the quiet line underneath. This ships today with no asset work, which is the argument that settles it: the mono lockup is still worth commissioning for dark headers elsewhere, but it was never the fix for this screen.

The bar is the answer to the thing that actually kills emergency design: a situation that lasts four days. A takeover on day one is right and a takeover on day three is an app you delete. So urgency decays on a schedule the resident can feel – a takeover collapses to a bar after the first view or after 30 minutes, whichever comes first, and the bar collapses to a line. It never covers content, it pushes content, because anything that covers the page in a long situation gets swiped away reflexively and takes the real update with it. The one control that is not an action is "keep collapsed until the number changes": people will absolutely mute a four-day river, and the honest move is to give them a mute that re-arms itself rather than let them turn the whole thing off.

"What this actually means" is the most valuable thing on the screen and the fastest way to lose the town's trust. So the panel has a rule it cannot break: every line restates something in the document and names the page. No line may contain a judgement, a consequence we predict, a count of who is for and against, or a characterisation of anyone's motive. When the packet is silent, the panel says it is silent – "the ordinance does not name a tenant" is the single most useful line in that list, and it is pure description. The disclosure is not buried: it names a human editor, because "the AI summarised it" is not an answer anyone in a council chamber will accept.

Moved to the 16 June agenda by voice vote. There is no roll to show, so we show none rather than inventing an eight-row table of dashes.

Vote records are ugly because they are built as spreadsheets, and a spreadsheet answers "what were the numbers" when the resident is asking "did it pass, and how did my ward vote." So the outcome is a word first and a tally second, the ward is printed beside every name because that is how a resident finds themselves in the list, and the roll is grouped by how people voted rather than sorted by seat. There is deliberately no red and no green: it is the one thing every civic data product reaches for and it does three bad things at once – it fails for colour blind readers, it makes a routine procedural vote look like a fight, and in this system red is spoken for. Sage marks the affirmative, a hollow ring marks the negative, and both are labelled in words, so the record survives being screenshotted into a group chat.

"Locked on" is the emergency channel. It renders as a switch so its state is legible, and it is disabled with an explanation rather than hidden, because a control you cannot find is worse than one you cannot move.

The default is urgent and up, quiet hours on, and it has to be conservative for a reason that is not politeness. Notification permission is spent once. A resident installs this in July because they want to know what is on this weekend; if the first three pushes are a planning commission agenda, a leaf pickup schedule, and a heat advisory, notifications are off by August and they are off in November when the ice storm comes. The subscription screen is therefore an argument for turning things on, made after the app has earned it, not a wall of switches at signup. Frequencies are printed next to every rung because "about 9 a week" is the only honest way to describe what a person is agreeing to, and it is the number that stops them picking Everything and regretting it. The one thing we do not let anyone turn off is the emergency channel, and we say so in a sentence instead of quietly enforcing it.

  • Severity is a word in the header, beside the app name, where iOS and Android both leave room. It is not in the title, because the title has one job.
  • The title is an instruction or a fact, never a teaser. "Tornado warning until 11:45 pm" is complete on its own. "Important weather update" is not a notification, it is an ad for one.
  • The body names the source in its last clause so a person deciding whether to believe it can, without unlocking.
  • No emoji, no siren glyph, no all caps. The escalation is carried by the words and by the fact that it arrived at all.

Informational never pushes
There is no informational push specimen because there is no informational push. Leaf pickup, agendas, and rate schedules appear in the app and in the weekly digest. Neutrality has to be built out of layout, not promised in a policy page nobody reads. Every candidate gets the same row, the same weight, the same one link, and the alphabet decides the order – there is no editorial surface left to be biased with. No photographs, because a photograph is a persuasive object and we cannot source eight of them evenly. No summaries of a candidate's positions, because writing six of those fairly is beyond what a town platform can defend, and writing five of them fairly while getting one wrong is worse than writing none. Ballot questions are printed as certified, word for word, since a paraphrase of a ballot title is an argument about it. The list of things this module will never do is on the screen and not in a policy document, because in a town of 29,000 the person who thinks we tilted it is going to be standing in front of us at the farmers market.

Every civic item carries where it came from and when we got it, because the whole product is a claim about someone else's document. The line is deliberately the quietest thing in the card: it is there for the one reader in fifty who wants it, and for the argument in the comments that it settles in four words.
