Creations

The build layer: small apps residents make and ship into Town Square. Each one is a static bundle running in a sandboxed iframe on a different domain, talking to the shell through a postMessage SDK and inheriting the town's design tokens. This section covers the gallery, the cards, the seam between our chrome and their code, the trust sheet, write-back consent, failure, and the invitation to build one.

A gallery of resident-built apps has one job that an app store does not have: it has to make the person scrolling it think I could build one of these. So it is not arranged by popularity and it does not lead with icons. It leads with a story about a person, because the gap a resident is actually trying to cross is not technical, it is permissional. Marta is a library clerk. If Marta did it, the thought goes, the door is open. Everything else on the screen is downstream of that: every card carries a human name in the same weight as the app name, the counts are neighbors who used it rather than downloads, and the build invitation is a quiet permanent fixture rather than a banner that appears once. An app store sells you things other people made. This is a town noticeboard for things your neighbors made, and the difference has to be visible in three seconds.

Build one

Describe what you want to the AI tool you already use, connect it to Town Square, and a neighbor on the review committee reads it before it goes up. Most of these took a weekend.

Nobody is paid for anything on this page. The source of every creation is public and you can fork any of them.

What the gallery refuses to be

Not an app store. No stars, no five-across icon grid, no ranked chart, no install button. Nothing here is bought, so nothing here is merchandised.

Not a list of links. Every entry carries four facts a link cannot: what it does, who made it, how many neighbors used it, and what it touches. The touch strip is the load-bearing one, and it is on the card, not one tap away.

Not sorted by winner. The featured slot is the most used this month, so it rotates. A permanent leaderboard tells a new builder the slots are taken.

The metric

Downloads measure a decision made before the thing was used. Stars measure who felt strongly. Neighbors who used it this month is the only number that means the app is still alive, and it decays honestly when a creation goes stale.

  1. 1
    Host chrome. Ours.

    Rendered by the shell, outside the frame. The name, the maker and the back button are ours to write, and a creation cannot reach a single pixel of this strip.

  2. 2
    The badge. The load-bearing pixel.

    The one piece of chrome a creation can never draw over. That is what makes it structurally unable to imitate Town Square: a resident always has a frame of reference the guest cannot touch, so a fake sign-in drawn inside the frame still sits under a badge that says the thing below it is somebody's bundle. There is no login form anywhere inside a creation for one to imitate, and there never will be.

  3. 3
    The seam. A different registrable domain.

    Not a policy, the cookie jar. The session cookie is structurally unable to reach the guest and the browser puts it in another process. The only channel across is one MessagePort, and the bridge checks every call against the frozen manifest on the host side, so a hand-written message that skips the SDK still gets permission_denied.

  4. 4
    The creation. Theirs.

    Luis's HTML, Luis's CSS, Luis's judgment about what a burrito score means. It looks native because the host injects the town's design tokens as CSS custom properties, so var(--ts-terracotta) resolves for him too and costs him nothing to adopt. A town that reskins Town Square reskins every creation with it.

  5. 5
    Footer chrome. Ours, and deliberately below.

    Report sits outside the frame so a resident reporting a creation is always talking to Town Square and never to the thing they are reporting. Putting it inside would be the single most obvious hole in the design.

Ownership is carried by a label and an edge treatment, never by color alone: ours has a solid navy rule and the word Town Square, theirs has a dashed terracotta outline and the word Guest bundle. The dashes are doing real work here, because the seam is the one boundary in the product where being unsure which side you are on is a security failure rather than a cosmetic one.

The alarming manifest gets no red, no warning triangle and no scare copy. It gets a count, a truthful qualifier (two of them are not maps), the primary action demoted to sit beside Not now, and one sentence saying it is fine to walk away. Making this screen frightening would teach residents to fear the trust sheet rather than read it, and the sheet is only useful if they read it every time. The tally line at the top is the whole design: it makes the sheet skimmable without making it skippable.

Write-back consent attribution is automatic and cannot be forged, so the sheet says so rather than asking you to trust it

This is the moment the build layer actually compounds: a creation asking to put a fact into the town under a resident's name. Three things make the sheet honest. It shows the payload, field by field, because "allow Flock Camera Map to write records" is a sentence nobody can evaluate. Attribution is stated as a property of the platform, not a promise: the builder never touches it, which is precisely why it cannot be forged. And there is no delete, so the sheet says that out loud rather than letting a resident assume an undo exists. Revert is a platform operation the committee performs; a creation cannot invoke one.

Why the payload is shown raw

Field names in mono, values in prose. A resident does not need to know what lng means to see that this creation is sending four things and not forty, and a builder debugging a schema gets the same screen. One surface, two readers.

Scope of "always allow"

Per creation and per record type. A creation that later declares a second type has to ask again, which closes the obvious hole: get a broad grant while you are harmless, then widen what you write.

What is deliberately missing

No "remember for all creations". A blanket write grant across the build layer would make the consent sheet a formality within a week, and the sheet is the only thing standing between a resident's name and forty independently written apps.

The sheet is ours

Drawn by the shell over the frame, with the eyebrow saying so. The creation asked; Town Square is the one doing the asking.

No rating, anywhere

A five-star average on a thing your neighbor made for free is a small cruelty and a bad signal. The page carries usage, contributor count and version history instead, all of which are facts rather than opinions.

Version history is a permissions history

Each entry says whether the ask grew, and who reviewed it. Publish something harmless, build an audience, then push an update that asks for more is the browser-extension attack, and it works precisely where updates are rubber-stamped. Permission expansion always forces a human, and the history makes that visible after the fact too.

Showing the schema and the two creations already reading it is how compounding becomes legible. A resident sees their contribution feeding somebody else's app, and a builder sees a type they can use instead of inventing a parallel one.

The build layer

You do not need permission, and you do not need to be a programmer.

Twelve people in Russellville have built something on Town Square. Two of them had written code before.

  1. 1
    Tell your AI tool what you want Claude, ChatGPT, whatever you already use. "A map of the free little libraries in town, where anybody can add one."
  2. 2
    Point it at Town Square One connection, one time. Your tool then knows the whole contract: what it can read, what it can write, and how to ask.
  3. 3
    Send it over A neighbor on the committee reads what it asks for and what it does. Usually a few days. They will tell you what to fix rather than just saying no.
  4. 4
    It is in the town Running sandboxed, in the town's own type and color, with your name on it. Anything it records becomes something the next person can build on.

I thought you had to be a programmer. I described it out loud, the same way I would explain it to somebody at the desk, and it mostly wrote itself. The part I did was knowing what the town needed.

MK Marta Keener, Flock Camera Map

Free, and it stays yours. Nobody is paid for anything in the gallery, and Town Square takes no cut of anything, because there is nothing to take a cut of.

What makes this not a growth prompt

It never interrupts. The interstitial is reached by tapping How it works, never shown on a timer, a visit count, or after a resident finishes something. A modal nobody asked for is the exact move this section is defined against.

No counter, no scarcity, no reward. No "join 340 builders", no launch deadline, no badge at the end. The offer is that a small thing you want to exist can exist, and the honest cost is a weekend and a neighbor reading your code.

It names the hard part correctly. Step one is describing the thing, and the pull quote says the work was knowing what the town needed. Telling a library clerk that the coding is easy is only credible if you also tell her which part is not.

It admits the wait. Review takes a few days and the page says so. A build funnel that hides its slowest step gets one enthusiastic resident and then never sees them again.

The one dark screen

The interstitial inverts because it is the only full-screen interruption in the whole section, and because the featured card has already trained the eye that dark means this is the build layer. Two dark surfaces in the section, both about the same idea.
