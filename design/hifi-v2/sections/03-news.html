News, the feed, and the reader

Town Square aggregates a handful of real sources and links out to every one of them. We never host the article. That single constraint decides the lead, the row, the cluster and the reader page: our job is to be the fastest honest route to somebody else's reporting, and to be legible on the mornings when the town published nothing at all.

The four are a ladder, resolved in one pass: developing outranks everything if a story is still moving, then photograph if the source shipped art, then pull-quote if an editor marked a line, then typographic. This is the pattern local aggregation gets wrong most often, and the failure is always the same: three rows, three headlines, one event, and a reader who concludes the feed repeats itself. Collapsing to a single row and picking a winner is worse. It hides that the radio station has the tape and the blogger was in the room, and it quietly ranks a daily paper above a person writing on her own, which is exactly the judgement an aggregator has no standing to make. So the cluster keeps every source, at the same size, ordered by time and nothing else. Our one sentence at the top is the event, written plainly and labelled as ours, because passing a synthesised headline off as a publisher's is the fastest way to lose the sources we depend on. The medium tag matters more than it looks: Reported, Audio and Opinion let a reader pick what they actually want in about a second, and it is the one piece of editorial judgement we can make without stepping on anyone.

The skeleton is drawn from the same rules as the feed, not from a generic set of grey bars: the lead art is 200px because the lead art is 200px, the third row has no thumbnail because roughly half of stories have none, and the section labels are real text, immediately because we always know what the sections are before we know what is in them. This page has to be useful to a reader and respectful of a publisher in the same breath, and those two pull in opposite directions: everything that makes it a better read makes it a better substitute for the article. The resolution is a hard ceiling. Three sentences and three points, never more, set in a panel that is visibly ours and labelled Town Square summary, with the line underneath saying plainly that we have nothing The Courier does not. The photograph runs at thumbnail scale with a credit and a link, not full bleed. The only prominent control on the screen is the one that leaves. A reader who wants the story goes and gets it from the people who did the work, and the summary is enough to decide whether to spend the click, which is the actual job.

Why am I seeing this is on every story, not buried in settings, because the honest answer is short and it is the answer people assume they will not get. Newest first inside the topics you chose. No profile, no engagement score, no paid placement. Writing it down where a resident meets it costs a disclosure and buys the only thing an aggregator has, which is being believed.

The rule that makes this work is that every entry is one sentence about what changed, not a headline. Headlines stacked in date order are just the feed again with a border round it. "The city posted Tuesday's agenda, the rezoning is item 9" is the thing a resident following this actually needs, and it is a sentence we can write honestly from a public document without touching anyone's copy. A town with nothing to report is the normal case, not the error case, and the difference between those two framings is the whole tone of the product. The quiet day screen never apologises and never pads. It says what is true, it says why that is fine, and then it does the one genuinely useful thing available, which is offering yesterday in a compact list for somebody who was busy. What it does not do is invent a section. A screen that fills itself out on a slow Wednesday teaches people it was padded on the Wednesday the river came up, and that is a lesson a town information service does not get to unteach.

Relative time, the whole rule set We count minutes for exactly one hour, then switch to the words a neighbour would use. Nobody says "seven hours ago" out loud.
Age Renders as Why
Under a minute just now A number this small is noise. It means the page you are looking at is current.
1 to 59 minutes 18 minutes ago The only place we count, and the only timestamp allowed to go dark and bold. Inside an hour, the number changes what a resident does next.
Same day, over an hour this morning Also this afternoon and tonight, cut at noon and 6pm. Everyone knows what this morning means without doing arithmetic against their own watch.
Yesterday yesterday afternoon Keeps the part of day, because "yesterday afternoon" and "yesterday, 11:04 pm" answer different questions about a council vote.
2 to 6 days Tuesday A weekday name inside the last week is unambiguous and it is what people say. No "4 days ago".
7 days to a year 3 Jun Past a week the relative form stops helping. This is a date, and it is short enough to sit in a byline.
Over a year 3 Jun 2025 The year appears only when it is doing work, which is the moment a saved story could be mistaken for a current one.
Anything developing updated 12 minutes ago A live story keeps the minute count past the hour and takes the word updated, because on a developing story the question is not when it started, it is when it last moved.
On a scheduled item Monday, 6pm Anything in the future is an absolute time, always. A meeting "in 3 days" is how somebody misses a meeting.

Every timestamp in the section is serif and sits in the byline, never in a chip and never in mono. The rule set exists because inconsistent time is the fastest way for an aggregator to feel untrustworthy: a feed showing 2 hours ago beside Tuesday beside 08/04 tells a reader the screen was assembled by three different people who never met. One ladder, one voice, and the full timestamp is always on the element's title and in the reader page byline for anyone who needs the real number.
