Events, the calendar, and what’s on this weekend

Everything here is scraped from the city, Arkansas Tech, the Pope County Library, half a dozen churches, the Center for the Arts and the parks department. The data is messy by nature: missing end times, a postal address where a venue name should be, things that recur, things that get cancelled at four in the afternoon. These components are designed against that reality rather than against a clean seed dataset.

The four category tints already ship and they are tokens, not shades picked to look nice beside the last one: civic is gold, music is sage, sport is terracotta, community is navy. Everything below extends that vocabulary rather than adding to it. The fifth state is the honest one, an event the categoriser would not confidently place, and it gets no colour at all.

Three things carry it, and none of them is colour alone. The clock is hatched over, because the clock is the promise that broke. The category tint is withdrawn to inert stone, because a tint is a claim that something is happening. The venue line is replaced by who cancelled it and when. The title stays full weight and full black.

Cancellation is the most common correction in the feed, so it cannot use brick. Brick is the one alarm register and spending it on a rained-out market is how it stops meaning anything at 6am when the river is up. Withdrawal reads as loudly as alarm here and costs nothing.

Why it resolves this way

The now line is a list item, not an overlay. It sits between two rows in document order, so it is announced in reading order and never lands on top of a title. It carries a real clock time because “now” on a screen someone left open for an hour is a lie.

The gap is written, not drawn. Four hours of nothing on a civic calendar is normal and should feel normal. A dotted line with “3 hr” on it reads as an error; a sentence reads as a town.

Earlier today collapses, it does not disappear. Three events that already happened are still the answer to “did I miss it”, which is a question people ask this screen constantly.

Tomorrow is on the same scroll. At nine in the evening, today is over and the honest answer to “what’s on” is tomorrow morning. A day view that dead ends at midnight makes the user navigate to learn nothing.

The one written line

The lede is set in Newsreader at 15.5px, the only editorial voice on the screen, and it is the single element on this whole surface that a person writes. Everything below it is scraped.

It is worth the effort because it is the difference between a digest and a dump. “Move in weekend at Tech, so the whole town is busier than it wants to be” is a thing a neighbour says. No aggregator generates that, and a resident can tell within one sentence which one they are reading.

Saturday gets a card, Friday and Sunday get rows. The weekend has one obvious marquee thing and ten ordinary ones, and flattening that into eleven identical cards throws away the only editorial judgement in the product.

Counts are on the day heading, so a light Sunday is stated rather than implied by a short list, which could equally mean the scrape failed.

Why it resolves this way

Plain words first. “Saturday morning, this weekend” is the answer to the question actually being asked. The exact stamp sits directly under it for the person who is putting it in a calendar, and neither has to be hunted for.

The moved notice is above the when, not below it. It is gold, the civic and notices register, and it says what changed and where it changed from. A resident who drove to Depot Park last August needs the old location named, not a corrected address with no history.

Recurrence is stated twice on purpose: once as a chip beside the category, once as a date bounded sentence. “Every Saturday” with no end date is how a calendar quietly fills with things that stopped happening in October.

The source line is not a footnote. Everything on this screen came from somebody else’s website, and saying so, with the time we last looked, is the whole basis for trusting the rest of it.

Why it resolves this way

Four fields and one of them is optional. Every field past this point is a field that stops somebody submitting a fish fry. Category, cost, end time and description are all things we can infer, ask later, or live without.

The date field takes prose. A structured date picker is the single most abandoned control in a form like this on a phone. Parsing “next fri 6ish” and then showing our work is strictly better than making a resident operate a calendar widget.

The review notice is a promise, not a warning. It says who reads it, roughly when, and explicitly that we will not rewrite their words. Moderation that is not described in advance reads as censorship when it happens.

Identity stays pseudonymous. Verified means neighbours vouched for them, not that the town knows where they live. The listing shows the handle and the badge, and nothing else exists to show.

The skeleton is the agenda row’s own geometry: 46px gutter, 34px tile, two lines. Nothing reflows when the data lands.

These three are the same screen with no events on it, and the resident has to be able to tell them apart in under a second. Quiet is warm and names something else to do. Broken says which source failed, when we last had good data, what to do instead, and leaves the stale rows visible and marked rather than hiding them. Loading holds the exact shape of what is coming. The failure state gets gold and not brick: a city calendar timing out is a notice, not an emergency, and brick has to still mean something at 6am when the river is up.
