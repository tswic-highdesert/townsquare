Contribution, and the composer

Everything a resident puts into Town Square is a typed record with a shape and a purpose: an event, a correction to a listing, a notice from an organisation, a collection, a photo of something that happened. There is no box that says "what's on your mind". Every record is versioned, attributed and revertible, which is what makes light moderation survivable in a town this size.

Typed beats freeform because the type is the moderation policy. A general status box has no shape, so it accepts everything, and the thing it accepts most easily is a grievance about a neighbour. Nobody sets out to build a complaint board. You build a box, and the box decides. Asking for the shape first does three useful things at once: it turns "someone should do something about the potholes on Weir Road" into either an event, a correction or nothing, it gives every record fields that make it findable in six months, and it means the rules can be per type rather than one global standard nobody agrees on. Corrections are checked against the listing they touch. Notices are gated on being an organisation. Photos allow no comments. None of that is possible when everything arrives as untyped text. The cost is real: a resident with something to say that fits no shape is turned away. We take that cost knowingly, and the chooser says so in plain words rather than hiding it.

The parse line is a question, not a result. A date picker is unambiguous and slow. Natural language is fast and occasionally wrong, and a wrong start time is the single most damaging error in this whole product: fifteen people stand in a car park at the wrong hour and nobody trusts the events feed again. So the input takes what someone would say out loud, then reads it back in full prose with the weekday spelled out, because "14/06" and "06/14" look identical to a person skimming and "Saturday 14 June" does not. The ambiguous state deliberately picks nothing. Defaulting to pm would be right most of the time in a town with evening ball games, and wrong every Saturday morning at the market, which is exactly the population that matters most.

The gate is written as a redirect because the person hitting it is almost always right and just in the wrong door. Somebody who wants to post a notice has something to tell the town. Refusing them and stopping there teaches them the product is closed. So the gate names the actual reason, offers the route that solves it, and then lists the three things they probably meant instead, in the order they are likely. The third one sends them to the phone, which is the correct answer and the one a community platform is usually too proud to give. On severity: the failure mode here is not malice, it is a kind secretary who genuinely believes the supper time matters urgently, and it does, to her. So the consequence is written in physical terms, "wakes up every phone in Russellville, tonight, including people asleep", rather than in policy terms. The date of the last real use is shown because it is the most persuasive sentence on the screen.

EXIF is the obvious leak, and telling people about it is the point. We built a whole identity model on never storing an address, and then the first photo somebody uploads from their front porch carries the coordinates of their front porch inside the file. Stripping it silently would be correct and invisible. The rejection is the most important screen in this section. It is the only moment where the platform tells a resident no, and how it reads decides whether they ever contribute again. So it is a person writing to a person: it names what was wrong with the record and never with them, it says exactly what would make it acceptable, it commits to acting quickly if they do, and it gives them a way to argue including a phone number. It is signed "a moderator" rather than by name, because in a town of 29,000 a named moderator gets stopped in the Kroger car park, and that is how you lose your only moderator. The brick alarm colour is nowhere near this screen. Being turned down is not an emergency.

Step two is the one that does the real work. It makes a person notice their own volume without stopping them, and in testing that is usually enough.

The limit is explained by its threat model, not by its number. "You have reached your daily limit" is a sentence that makes a person feel suspected, and the person hitting it is nearly always the one running the market booth or the school calendar. So the copy names the actual thing being defended, a compromised account posting two hundred fake events overnight, and admits straight out that the rule has to apply to everyone or it defends nobody. It then lists what still works, because the most useful sentence in a rate limit screen is the one that says the work is not lost. And the escalation path is a message to a person, not a form, which is a thing you can only offer in a product built for one town.

The whole section is one argument: a record you can fix cheaply does not need to be policed expensively. Every design choice here leans on the same two lines in the data model. Because a version is never destroyed, a correction from a stranger is low stakes, so corrections can be open to any verified resident instead of to owners only. Because attribution is dual, a creation writing on a resident's behalf can be cut off without deleting the resident's work, so residents can safely let their apps write. Because revert is a forward operation, a moderator can undo something in one tap and the contributor can put it back, which is why the revert screen can be written kindly and mean it. Take versioning out and every one of those choices flips to the defensive version: locked fields, owner-only edits, a review queue in front of everything, and a product nobody bothers to contribute to.
